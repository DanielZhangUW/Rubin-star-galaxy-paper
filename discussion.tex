\section{Discussion and Conclusions}
\label{sec:discussion}

Reliable separation of unresolved and resolved sources is important for a wide range of Rubin
science applications. Stellar population studies require samples with controlled galaxy
contamination and quantified completeness, while extragalactic analyses require the complementary
suppression of foreground stars. These requirements become increasingly difficult to satisfy at
faint magnitudes, where the stellar and galaxy morphology distributions overlap and galaxies
outnumber stars by more than an order of magnitude. A continuous probabilistic description is
therefore more informative than a binary morphology flag because it retains the uncertainty
associated with each classification and allows independent measurements to be combined before a
catalog assignment is made \citep[][SIL2020]{Henrion2011BayesianSG}.


LSST photometric catalogs provide several classifiers, including a per-band binary classifier,
\texttt{extendedness}, based on a threshold in the magnitude difference, $\Delta m$, between PSF
($m_{\mathrm{PSF}}$) and CModel ($m_{\mathrm{CModel}}$) magnitudes, an approach pioneered by
SDSS. The theoretical reasons why this morphological classifier should be at least as effective as
any other proposed in the literature are discussed at length in SIL2020. Despite the widespread use
of $\Delta m$ based on CModel magnitudes in thousands of SDSS papers, we demonstrated that {\it
  superior classification performance can be achieved by simply replacing CModel magnitudes with
  S\'{e}rsic magnitudes} in the definition of $\Delta m$. A detailed investigation of why S\'{e}rsic
magnitudes are better suited than CModel magnitudes for star-galaxy separation is beyond the scope
of this work. One possible clue is the ``pile-up'' of galaxies around $\Delta m \sim 0$ at the faint
end when CModel magnitudes are used (see Figure~\ref{fig:magdmcomparison}). We note that the SDSS pipelines
\citep{2002SPIE.4836..350L} did not fit S\'{e}rsic models with a free S\'{e}rsic index $n$, but
instead used a linear combination of exponential and de Vaucouleurs profiles with fixed $n=1$ and
$n=4$. Perhaps S\'{e}rsic magnitudes would also have performed better than CModel magnitudes in SDSS
had they been available.


Nevertheless, a magnitude-independent threshold for $\Delta m$ is not an optimal approach to
star-galaxy separation, even when S\'{e}rsic magnitudes are used. The main shortcomings of this
approach are its inability to combine morphological information from different bands in a
theoretically sound way and the lack of information about the resulting sample contamination and
completeness. To address these shortcomings, we implemented the Bayesian star-galaxy classification
method of SIL2020.


In each band, we model the distribution of sources in the two-dimensional space defined by $\Delta m
= m_{\mathrm{PSF}} - m_{\mathrm{Sersic}}$ and magnitude $m_{\mathrm{Sersic}}$ as a two-component
mixture model. Given the large number of objects, the two components, corresponding to unresolved
and resolved sources, can be constrained without using external information such as HST-based
labels. Once the best-fit model is available, sample contamination and completeness can be readily
computed, and their variation with magnitude can be controlled by tuning the sample selection
criteria (see \S\ref{sec:userCC}). Our results can be extended to fields closer to the Galactic
plane. However, the contamination levels for stellar samples discussed here for the high-latitude COSMOS field can
already be interpreted as upper limits for fields closer to the plane.


Thanks to the switch from CModel to S\'{e}rsic magnitudes and the probabilistic combination of
morphological information from multiple ($griz$) bands, the resulting classifier significantly
outperforms the default Rubin \texttt{extendedness} parameter. The improvement is especially notable
for stellar selection at the faint end (``galaxies are easy'': 97\% accuracy is achieved simply by
declaring all objects to be resolved). At the same level of stellar-sample contamination, the
stellar sample extends {\it more than a magnitude fainter} and approaches the idealized statistical
limit discussed by SIL2020. We also showed how stellar classification performance can be improved
using judiciously chosen color cuts designed to exclude contaminating galaxies.


This improved performance provides a strong argument for modifying and augmenting the star-galaxy
classification parameters provided to users in Rubin/LSST catalogs. At the very least,
\texttt{extendedness} should be based on $\Delta m$ computed using S\'{e}rsic magnitudes (see
Figure~\ref{fig:magdmCCcomparison} for a performance comparison). A more substantial, but highly valuable, addition
would be to compute $D_{griz}$ for every object and provide the expected stellar-sample completeness
and contamination if that value is used as the selection threshold. These parameters
could also be made available through post-processing as a value-added catalog because they are based
entirely on cataloged PSF and S\'{e}rsic fluxes.


The best-fit $\Delta m$ versus $m$ and $D_{griz}$ models are not universal and should vary with sky
location and image depth. The fitting algorithm was tested here on coadded Rubin images from the
COSMOS field, which are deeper than single-visit images (the $5\sigma$ detection limit for point
sources in the $r$ band is 26.4). It may be more difficult to constrain the $\Delta m$ distribution
of galaxies at brighter magnitudes in shallower, lower-S/N data. In addition, stars will eventually
dominate the sample closer to the Galactic plane, and the algorithmic steps may need to be
reoptimized. We leave these considerations for future work.


We have also tested star-galaxy separation based on the novel \texttt{griz\_model\_extendedness}
parameter. Although based on an ad hoc approach, it performs remarkably well (see \S\ref{sec:comparegriz}).
Its main practical shortcoming is that it is not a calibrated probability that an object is a star or a
galaxy, and does not allow users to estimate sample completeness and contamination without
external labels.


Finally, we note that a similar approach to star-galaxy classification, based on modeling $\Delta m$
versus magnitude distributions, was discussed by \cite{2021PASP..133e4502M}. The main difference
between their method and ours is that they used a KDE (kernel density estimation) method, which
requires an external training sample. Their Pan-STARRS1 dataset was approximately three magnitudes
shallower and therefore cannot be directly compared with our results.
