
\section{Star-galaxy Separation Methodology}

We first briefly review the Bayesian star-galaxy separation method proposed in SIL2020 and then
apply it to single-band Rubin data, followed by its extension to the multi-band case. Our principal
measured object property is the difference between PSF, \(m_{\mathrm{PSF}}\), and model  \(m_{\mathrm{model}}\),
magnitudes, \(\Delta m = m_{\mathrm{PSF}} - m_{\mathrm{model}}\). For unresolved sources, this \(\Delta m\) is consistent with zero within the measurement noise, while for resolved objects \(\Delta m > 0\) (see the top left panel in Figure~\ref{fig:truth_morphology_color}).
A detailed comparison and statistical discussion of this quantity and several other quantities proposed for
star-galaxy separation in the literature is available in SIL2020. 



\subsection{Which model magnitudes to use: CModel or  S\'{e}rsic?} 

Motivated by the successful use of \(\Delta m \) based on CModel magnitudes in thousands to SDSS papers,
and the fact that \texttt{extendedness} listed in Rubin catalogs is also based on this type of model
magnitudes, we initially considered this option, too. A performance comparison with 
\texttt{griz\_model\_extendedness} parameter revealed that replacing CModel magnitudes by S\'{e}rsic
magnitudes results in much improved star-galaxy separation (see Figure~\ref{fig:magdmCCcomparison}). 
For pedagogical reasons, all plots presented here are based on S\'{e}rsic magnitudes but we have
produced both versions of the entire analysis.

Both CModel and S\'{e}rsic magnitudes/fluxes are based on fits of the S\'{e}rsic brightness profile
(e.g., \citealt{2005PASA...22..118G})
\begin{equation}
          I(R)=I_e\exp\left\{ -b_n\left[ \left(\frac{R}{R_e}\right)^{1/n}-1 \right]\right\}.
\end{equation}
to data.  Here $I(R)$ is the surface brightness at projected radius $R$; $R_e$ is the effective
radius enclosing half of the total light; $b_n$ is a normalization constant chosen so that $R_e$
encloses half of the total flux; $I_e$ is the surface brightness at the effective radius, and 
$n$ is the S\'ersic index. The S\'{e}rsic index controls the concentration and shape of the profile.
An exponential profile with $n=1$ is often used to describe galaxy disks. A profile with $n=4$, known
as de Vaucouleurs profile, is often used to describe elliptical galaxies and classical bulges.

The main difference between  CModel vs. S\'{e}rsic fluxes is that the latter are based
on best-fit profile where $n$ is a free model parameter, while CModel fluxes are based
on a linear combination of two best-fit S\'{e}rsic profiles where $n$ is fixed to 1 and 4 (``C''
stands for composite\footnote{For more details, see https://www.sdss3.org/dr8/algorithms/magnitudes.php}), 
The code takes the best fit exponential and de Vaucouleurs profile fits in a given band and asks for
the linear combination of the two that best fits the image. The weighting coefficient (clipped between zero
and one) is defined as de Vaucouleurs term, fracDeV, defining the CModel composite flux as 
\begin{equation}
         F_{CModel}  = fracDeV \, F_{deV} + (1 - fracDeV) \, F_{exp}. 
\end{equation}

Figure~\ref{fig:magdmcomparison} shows \(\Delta m \) vs. \(m_{\mathrm{CModel}}\) and
\(\Delta m \) vs. \(m_{\mathrm{Sersic}}\) distributions, separately for unresolved and resolved
objects classified using HST labels.  Two features are noteworthy. First, HST galaxies 
dominate the region around $\dm \sim 0$ at the faint end ($r > 24$) much more strongly
in case of CModel magnitudes and are likely explanation for its inferior performance at
the faint end. Nevertheless, the distribution of \(\Delta m \) for unresolved sources based
on S\'{e}rsic magnitudes is much broader and with a median that deviates more from zero
than in case of CModel magnitudes. This behavior would result in worse star-galaxy
separation performance with S\'{e}rsic magnitudes but only when a simple cut on \(\Delta m \)
is used for classification. With explicit modeling of these distributions, described below,
\(\Delta m \) based on S\'{e}rsic magnitudes is clearly superior to its alternative based
on CModel magnitudes, especially at the faint end of the sample.

\subsection{Bayesian Star-Galaxy Separation Method from SIL2020}

We first focus on the single-band case and then extend our discussion to the multi-band case.
Using realistic simulations, SIL2020 showed that adopting a fixed, predefined threshold for $\Delta m$ to select resolved objects is not optimal. The threshold should depend on magnitude because of variations in the object populations and signal-to-noise ratio, as well as on sky location because the galaxy-to-star count ratio varies across the sky. Moreover, for an optimal combination of information from different bands or from other classification methods (e.g., based on colors), the observed $\Delta m$ distribution needs to be transformed into a proper probability density function. 

The subsample of objects with HST labels can be used to quantify the $\Delta m$ distribution separately
for unresolved and resolved objects (see Figure~\ref{fig:magdmcomparison}). However, such a ``training'' sample is currently available only
for a very small fraction of the LSST footprint (less than 1\% of the area).
In Steps~1 and~2, we use HST labels across the full COSMOS footprint to identify the
reference stars and galaxies whose Rubin measurements determine the two profiles.
In the \(1^\circ\times1^\circ\) field, we hold these profiles fixed and fit only the
stellar fraction \(\omega_U(m)\) using unlabeled Rubin measurements. We use HST labels
again to measure completeness and contamination for comparison with the predictions. 



\subsection{Mixture Model for \(\Delta m\) vs. \(m_{\mathrm{Sersic}}\) diagram \label{sec:mixmodel}} 

We assume that a sample is selected from a suitably chosen sky region over which the stellar and galaxy populations can be assumed to be statistically uniform. Over angular scales of
several degrees, stellar counts vary much more rapidly than galaxy counts (large galaxy clusters are
an exception, but not important for the subsequent discussion). Based on the analysis of state-of-the-art
Milky Way stellar population models by \cite{2025AJ....169..119P}, a sky region of several square
degrees, corresponding to Rubin tracts\footnote{In the Rubin Science Pipelines, tracts and patches
are the two levels of spatial subdivision used for coadded images and object catalogs. They are
designed to make processing and storage scalable while preserving continuity across the sky.
A tract is a relatively large region of the sky, approximately 1.75$^\circ$ $\times$ 1.75$^\circ$ (the exact
size depends on the sky map projection). that is processed as a unit. Tracts overlap slightly with
neighboring tracts to avoid edge effects. Each tract is subdivided into a regular grid of patches,
with each tract in DP2 containing 81 patches. The patch size is about 12 arcmin $\times$ 12 arcmin
(approximately, one CCD).}  is sufficiently small for this purpose (and consistent with our choice of
the COSMOS field for quantitative analysis). 
 
Given a ``tract'' catalog, at a fixed S\'{e}rsic magnitude (see below for binning details), the
distribution of \(\Delta m\) in a given band (with the band index suppressed for notational simplicity) is
modeled as a mixture of an unresolved (star) component and a resolved (galaxy) component, 
\begin{equation}
                                p(\dm \,|\, m) = w_U(m) \, \funr (\dm \,|\, m) +  w_R(m) \, \fres (\dm \,|\, m),
  \label{eq:mixpdf1}
\end{equation}
where $w_U + w_R = 1$, and $\funr$  and $\fres$  are properly normalized probability density
functions. With this decomposition, the probability that an object is unresolved is given by
Bayes' theorem as
\begin{equation}
        p_S(\dm) \equiv p({\rm unr}\mid\dm)= \frac{w_U(m) \funr(\dm \,|\, m)}  {w_U(m) \funr (\dm \,|\, m) + w_R(m) \fres (\dm \,|\, m)}.
  \label{eq:pSdm} 
\end{equation}
and the probability that it is resolved is $p_G = 1 - p_S$ (note that we use indices $S$ and
$unr$, or $G$ and $res$ interchangeably). 

The same model predicts the performance, completeness and contamination, for each class
as a function of a hard $\dm$ threshold. If $\dm<\dm_{\rm cut}$ is classified as unresolved (S),
\begin{equation}
      {\rm Completeness}(S)=   F_{\rm unr}(\dm_{\rm cut}) =  \int_{-\infty}^{\dm_{\rm cut}}\funr (\dm) d\dm,
      \label{eq:complS} 
\end{equation}                                       
and
\begin{equation}
       {\rm Contamination}(S) = \frac{w_R  F_{\rm res}(\dm_{\rm cut}) }
       {w_U  F_{\rm unr}(\dm_{\rm cut}) + w_R  F_{\rm res}(\dm_{\rm cut})}.
       \label{eq:contS} 
\end{equation}
We test these predictions below using HST-based labels.
% However, one needs to be careful because HST labels are not perfect.
% There are HST stars that appear resolved in Rubin and have non-stellar colors (quasars, close
%  binary stars) and some HST galaxies are cannot be resolved using Rubin images obtained with
%  ground-based seeing.



We write \(x=\Delta m\). The stellar profile \(U(x\mid m)\equiv\funr(x\mid m)\)
combines a Johnson \(S_U\) core \(J\) and a shifted lognormal tail \(T\),
\begin{equation}
 U(x\mid m)=[1-f(m)]\,J(x\mid m)+f(m)\,T(x\mid m).
 \label{eq:mixpdf2}
\end{equation}
Here \(f(m)\) is the fraction of the stellar probability in the tail.
Writing \(z=(x-\mu)/\sigma_c\) and letting \(\phi\) and \(\Phi\) denote
the standard normal density and cumulative distribution function, we use
\begin{equation}
 J(x\mid m)=\frac{\delta}{\sigma_c\sqrt{1+z^2}}\,
       \phi\!\left(\gamma+\delta\operatorname{asinh}z\right).
\end{equation}
The tail shares the same location \(\mu\) and has scale \(\sigma_c\kappa\),
\begin{equation}
 T(x\mid m)=
 \begin{cases}
 0 & x\leq\mu,\\[.2em]
 \displaystyle\frac{1}{h(x-\mu)}\,
    \phi\!\left[\frac{1}{h}\ln\!\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma_c\kappa}\right)\right]
     & x>\mu.
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:mixpd4}
\end{equation}
The seven fitted stellar quantities and the calculation of \(\mu\),
\(\kappa\), and the tail peak \(x_T\) are given in Appendix~\ref{app:v12-fitting}.

The galaxy profile \(R(x\mid m)\equiv\fres(x\mid m)\) combines two
skew-normal densities,
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 R(x\mid m)&=[1-v(m)]\,R_1(x\mid m)+v(m)\,R_2(x\mid m),\\
 R_j(x\mid m)&=\frac{2}{s_j}\phi(z_j)\Phi(\alpha_jz_j),\qquad
 z_j=\frac{x-\ell_j}{s_j}.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:mixpd5}
\end{equation}
Each component has location \(\ell_j\), scale \(s_j\), and skew
\(\alpha_j\). The fraction \(v(m)\) specifies the contribution of \(R_2\).

In Step~1, we fit \(U\) to Rubin measurements of HST reference stars.
In Step~2, we fit \(R\) to Rubin measurements of HST reference galaxies.
We use fits in magnitude bins to inspect the variation of the profile
parameters. The final smooth fits use each reference object's measured
\((m,x)\) directly. Each profile has seven fitted quantities, each
represented by a six-coefficient cubic spline (equation~\eqref{eq:v12-spline}).
In particular, the stellar tail fraction is
\begin{equation}
 f(m)=\frac{1}{1+\exp[-s_f(m)]},\qquad
 s_f(m)=\operatorname{logit}f(m),
 \label{eq:fmag}
\end{equation}
where \(s_f(m)\) is the spline fitted in Step~1. Step~2 represents
\(\operatorname{logit}v(m)\) in the same way.

In Step~3, we fit the stellar fraction \(w_U(m)\), written
\(\omega_U(m)\) in the Appendix, to the unlabeled Rubin calibration sample.
The fitted stellar fraction is
\begin{equation}
 w_U(m)=\sum_{j=0}^{6} w_j\binom{6}{j}q^j(1-q)^{6-j},
 \qquad q=(m-18)/8 .
 \label{eq:wUmag}
\end{equation}
The galaxy fraction is \(w_R(m)=1-w_U(m)\). We train the class
profiles in Steps~1 and~2 over the full COSMOS footprint. To apply them
in the \(1^\circ\times1^\circ\) COSMOS field, we use the same functions
and coefficients for \(U\) and \(R\), including those for \(f(m)\)
and \(v(m)\). The profiles still vary with magnitude. Step~3 optimizes
only the seven \(w_j\) using the unlabeled Rubin calibration sample in
the smaller field. We apply the same training and application procedure
to the \(D_{\mathrm{griz}}\) mixture. Appendix~\ref{app:v12-fitting}
gives the fitting details.

The quality of fits is illustrated in Figures~\ref{fig:slice_fits} and
\ref{fig:singleband-component-comparison}, which show the
mixture model overlaid on the observed \(\Delta m\) histograms in each magnitude bin, and in
Figure~\ref{fig:model2d_residuals}, which shows the 2D data, model, and residual maps in the
\(\Delta m\) vs.\ \(m_{\mathrm{S\acute{e}rsic}}\) plane. The model performs well in the most important
interval $\Delta m < 0.15$, where the probability that a source is resolved increase from zero to 
essentially unity. In particular, the bottom row in  Figure~\ref{fig:singleband-component-comparison}
shows good agreement of implied model components with subsets of data classified using HST
labels. Detailed agreement for $\Delta m > 0.15$ is not as important, as long as
the model is properly normalized so that estimates of stellar sample completeness and contamination
are reliable (see next section). 

The galaxy-to-star count ratio, which serves as Bayesian prior, is
\begin{equation}
                   N_{GS} \equiv   {N_G \over N_S}  = \frac{1-w_U(m)}{w_U(m)}. 
\end{equation}
The effectiveness of our fitting procedure is also demonstrated in
Figure~\ref{fig:star_galaxy_ratio}, which compares the implied galaxy-to-star count ratio
\(N_{\mathrm{gal}}/N_{\mathrm{star}}\) as a function of magnitude to counts of objects classified
using the COSMOS2020 external truth labels (note that in this sample galaxies outnumber stars
at the faint end by more than a factor of 30). When binary  \texttt{extendedness} parameter
is used for classification, a spurious turnover is seen at faint magnitudes. 

The expression in eq.~\ref{eq:pSdm} can be recast as
\begin{equation}
              p_S(\dm) =   \frac{1}  {1 +  N_{GS}  \, BF},
  \label{eq:pSbf} 
\end{equation}
where the Bayes factor (the likelihood ratio for the galaxy and star hypotheses) is
\begin{equation}
              BF = \frac{\fres} {\funr}. 
  \label{eq:bfdef} 
\end{equation}
With $N_{GS} \sim 30$ (see Figure~\ref{fig:star_galaxy_ratio}), it is sufficient to have $BF \sim 0.033$
for $p_S$ to drop below 0.5 (because the sample is highly imbalanced).  This decomposition in terms of prior
and Bayes factor is important later when combining multiple sources of classification information
(with the overall Bayes factor equal to the product of individual Bayes factors).

\subsection{The Classification Performance of the Single-band \(\Delta m\) Mixture Model}

Given the best-fit model for the data distribution in the $\Delta m$ vs. $m_{\mathrm{Sersic}}$
plane, each object is assigned a probability that it is unresolved, $p_S$ (or resolved, with
$p_G = 1 - p_S$). Given a chosen threshold for $p_S$, the sample can classified into two
classes, unresolved and resolved. Importantly, the best-fit model also predicts the class
completeness and contamination for the chosen $p_S$ threshold. 

Here we use HST labels to evaluate completeness and contamination for the stellar sample
and compare them with the mixture-model predictions. We focus on the stellar subsample and
the magnitude range, $22 < r < 26$. Brighter than this range, population mixing is much smaller and
measurements more accurate, leading to more accurate star-galaxy separation, whereas at fainter
magnitudes the performance deteriorates rapidly (especially for the stellar subsample).

Using the mixture model from eq.~\ref{eq:mixpdf1}, we compare 
completeness and contamination for the stellar subsample predicted by
eqs.~\ref{eq:complS} and \ref{eq:contS} with direct counts from the cleaned HST sample
in Figure~\ref{fig:singleband-prediction-comparison}.
The stellar and galaxy profiles come from the full-COSMOS fits to Rubin measurements
of HST reference objects. In the \(1^\circ\times1^\circ\) field, the Rubin fit holds
both profiles fixed and estimates only \(\omega_U(m)\) from unlabeled measurements.

Depending on particular science case, a user can control completeness or contamination
by choosing $p_S$ threshold. This optimization is discussed in more detail using better-performing
multiband classification parameter in \S\ref{sec:userCC}.

We expect classification to improve when $\dm$ measurements from multiple bands are used.
When combining information from different bands, a different approach is required than when
combining multiple measurements of the same type from a single band. The main reason is 
that it is sufficient for a source to be reliably identified as resolved in just one band, irrespective
of in how many other bands it was unresolved.  Therefore, combining
information from different bands is a nontrivial statistical problem that is made more complex
by astrophysical considerations. For example, galaxy morphology is wavelength dependent: 
different bands emphasize different stellar populations, dust distribution, star-forming regions,
bulges, and disks, and thus a galaxy can show strong resolved structure in one band while being
close to a PSF in another. A decisive measurement in one band should therefore not necessarily be
diluted by several bands with weak or absent evidence.  Consequently, combining measurements
from multiple bands is not as straightforward as combining multiple measurements of Bayes Factors
(see eq.~\ref{eq:bfdef}) from individual bands using eq.~\ref{eq:pSbf} (see Section 5 in SIL2020 for details). 

An optimal Bayesian approach would be to generalize eq.~\ref{eq:mixpdf1} to a vector form and
model the true object class as an underlying latent variable. The multi-band measurements are
correlated projections of this latent structure rather than independent measurements of one
scalar size. We explored a Gaussian copula construction for combining single-band probabilities,
but it did not provide a satisfactory description of the desired behavior. We therefore adopt a
different approach: first combine the measured $\dm$ values across bands into a single statistic
and then derive classification criteria from its distribution, analogously to the single-band case. 

Motivated by the fact that the multi-band $\dm$ distribution for the unresolved component can
be described as a multivariate nearly-Gaussian with diagonal covariance matrix, we define a standardized
variable in each band
\begin{equation} 
                             D_i = \frac{\dm_i-x_{\mathrm{peak},i}(m_i)}{\sigma_{U,i}(m_i)},
\end{equation}
where \(m_i\) is the S\'{e}rsic magnitude in band \(i\),
\(x_{\mathrm{peak},i}(m_i)\) is the peak of the full stellar profile \(U=J+T\),
and \(\sigma_{U,i}(m_i)\) is its characteristic width. Both functions are derived
from the full COSMOS reference stars as described in Appendix~\ref{app:v12-fitting}.
The utilized bands may be all of
$ugrizy$ or any subset of $N$ usable (depending on data depth and spectral energy distribution of
objects of interest) bands, $B$. We then define a radial metric
\begin{equation} 
                               D = \left( \sum_{i\in B} D_i^2 \right)^{1/2}. 
\end{equation}
When \(N=1\), \(D=|D_i|\) is the distance from the stellar peak in units of
\(\sigma_{U,i}\). If the standardized stellar deviations are independent
standard normal variables, \(D\) follows a \(\chi_N\) distribution. For \(N=1\),
this is the half-normal distribution. Residual offsets in the standardized
deviations give a noncentral \(\chi\) distribution under the same variance and
independence assumptions. For the central case with \(N>1\), the mean is
approximately \(\sqrt{N-1/2}\).
Depending on the desired completeness for the unresolved component, a hard $D$ threshold of
several times $\sqrt{N}$ can be used for sample classification (this is a standard case of
hypothesis testing in frequentist statistics). However, without a quantitative
description of the resolved component as well, the resulting sample contamination cannot be
estimated.

\subsection{Comparison of Multiband $D_{griz}$ Metric to \texttt{griz\_model\_extendedness} Parameter}
\label{sec:comparegriz}

Rubin (\citealt{2026arXiv261022613O}) introduced a novel star-galaxy separation metric
named \texttt{griz\_model\_extendedness}. The score is clipped to the interval [0, 1] but
it is not a calibrated probability that an object is a star or galaxy and should be 
be interpreted as a morphology ranking statistic. Given a subsets of bands, the algorithm
separately adds PSF and S\'{e}rsic fluxes and then utilizes these total fluxes to define
PSF a magnitude difference between PSF and S\'{e}rsic, as used here. In addition,
the algorithm applies an empirical calibration and an uncertainty correction, and then
modifies the result using a characteristic source size (for more details see Appendix B).
The last two modifications do not have a theoretical underpinning. Adding fluxes
from different bands is also not optimal (for example, in case of a barely resolved galaxy
in red bands with a strong blue AGN component this addition could mask the fact that
the source is a galaxy) unless galaxy morphology is strongly correlated between bands. 

We tested \texttt{griz\_model\_extendedness} using HST labels and it shows a
remarkable performance. Indeed, when trying to understand why its performance
is so superior to Rubin's default \texttt{extendedness} parameter, we realized that
the dominant reason is the use of  S\'{e}rsic flux instead of CModel magnitudes. 
We compare the contamination--completeness curves for our $D_{griz}$ classifier
and \texttt{griz\_model\_extendedness} classifier in Figure~\ref{fig:CCgrizCompare}.
Their relative performance depends on the completeness target. For
\(24.5\leq r<25\), at 20\% stellar completeness the contamination is 18.5\%
with \texttt{griz\_model\_extendedness} and 24.3\% with \(D_{\mathrm{griz}}\).
At approximately 50\% completeness, the corresponding values are 50.2\% and
43.9\%. We show in Appendix B that we can reproduce
\texttt{griz\_model\_extendedness} using cataloged parameters to apply uncertainty
and source size corrections.

We reiterate that \texttt{griz\_model\_extendedness} is not a calibrated probability that
an object is a star or galaxy, and by itself does not allow user to estimate completeness
and contamination for a selected threshold. Figure~\ref{fig:CCgrizCompareGrizmodel}
shows the variation of completeness and contamination for stellar selection using
three different values of \texttt{griz\_model\_extendedness} threshold.

As shown in preceeding sections, the performance of morphological separation of unresolved and
resolved sources starts to rapidly deteriorate about 2 magnitudes brighter than the $5\sigma$
limiting magnitude for point sources. Selection of unresolved objects is especially problematic
because the sample is highly imbalanced at the faint end. At the same time, samples of faint stars
are valuable for studying the Milky Way structure (e.g. stellar streams in the halo). Here we 
investigate if color selection can be used to decrease contamination without significant decrease
in completeness of such samples. 
 
Figure~\ref{fig:color-ccdiags} compares color distributions of morphologically selected samples
analyzed in preceeding sections. As evident, HST galaxies (misclassified as stars) appear
systematically bluer than stars, except for the $z-y$ color. Therefore, selection based on bluer
colors can result in improved classification.

Motivated by color distributions shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:color-ccdiags}, we investigate simple $g-r$ and $r-i$ selection criteria in
Figure~\ref{fig:color-performance}. The $g-r>0.3$ cut was chosen as appropriate for
selecting blue stars near the turn-off point on the principal stellar locus (composed of main
sequence and red giant stars; see e.g., \citealt{2026A&A...707A.333P}). The cut $g-r>1.0$ 
selects red (M type) stars and excludes almost all galaxies. The cut $r-i > 0.8$ is similar
but more effective at excluding galaxies.

The top row of Figure~\ref{fig:color-performance} compares $D_{griz}$
selections at the same stellar completeness, measured relative to all HST
reference stars in each magnitude bin. For $24\leq r<24.5$, contamination
at approximately 50\% completeness falls from 20.3\% without a color cut
to 17.8\% with $(g-r)_0>0.3$. For $24.5\leq r<25$, the same cut reduces
contamination from 24.3\% to 22.1\% when selecting 53 of 265 stars (20\%
completeness). At approximately 50\% completeness, the $D_{griz}$ threshold
needed to recover 133 stars rises from 6.02 to 6.49 after the color cut.
The number of selected galaxies falls from 104 to 87, reducing
contamination from 43.9\% to 39.5\%.

A stricter color cut can yield a cleaner sample at lower completeness.
In the fainter bin, $(g-r)_0>1.0$ combined with a $D_{griz}$ threshold
selects 53 stars and five galaxies, giving 8.6\% contamination at 20\%
completeness. The color cut itself retains 151 of the 265 reference stars,
so the maximum stellar completeness with this cut is 57.0\%.



\subsection{An Independent Method Test Using Euclid Data in the ECDFS Field}
\label{sec:ecdfs-check}

To test whether the COSMOS profiles can be transferred to another field,
we use separate calibration and evaluation regions in the Extended
Chandra Deep Field South (ECDFS). For classification
labels, we use the Euclid Data Release 1 (DR1) Deep Survey MER and PHZ classification catalogs from
IRSA\footnote{\href{https://irsa.ipac.caltech.edu/euclid-archive-documentation/data-access/\#catalogs}{IRSA Euclid DR1 catalog access}.}.
We positionally cross-matched Rubin DP2 objects to the MER catalog and retained mutual nearest neighbors
separated by less than 0.3 arcsec. We required a Euclid visible-band (VIS) detection, a zero
detection-quality flag, and a spurious-detection probability below 0.2. We join the PHZ catalog by
Euclid object ID and use \texttt{PHZ\_CLASSIFICATION} values of 1 for stars and 2 for galaxies.

We use the \(r\)-band stellar and galaxy profiles from Steps 1 and 2,
fitted over the full COSMOS footprint, as the starting point for both
ECDFS fits. The first fit calibrates those profiles using Rubin \(r\)
observations. The second uses the available Rubin \(g,i,z\) measurements
to refine that calibration. In the ECDFS transfer test, we
keep the full-COSMOS spline coefficients and estimate location and
scale corrections from unlabeled Rubin data before fitting \(w_U(m)\)
with the corrected profiles held fixed. Both fits estimate the stellar fraction
from unlabeled ECDFS Rubin data and assign stellar probabilities using
\(r\)-band measurements. We use Euclid DR1 labels only to evaluate
the fits in separate ECDFS sky regions.

Figure~\ref{fig:ecdfs-r-cc} compares both predictions with the
measurements from the same sample. Over the range from
\(20\%\) to \(80\%\) stellar completeness, the mean absolute
difference between predicted and measured contamination decreases
from 16.9 to 13.8 percentage points for \(24\leq r_{\mathrm{S\acute{e}rsic}}<24.5\)
and from 12.6 to 8.3 percentage points for
\(24.5\leq r_{\mathrm{S\acute{e}rsic}}<25\).
Appendix~\ref{app:v12-fitting} describes the calibration and gives the
numerical comparison.
